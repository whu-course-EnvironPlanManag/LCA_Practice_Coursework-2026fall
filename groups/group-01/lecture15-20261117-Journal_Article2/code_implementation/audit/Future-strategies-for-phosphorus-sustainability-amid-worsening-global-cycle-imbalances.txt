Significance 
Phosphorus (P) is intricately and 
inseparably associated with both 
food security and environmental 
sustainability. The comprehensive 
processes of the global 
anthropogenic P cycle, along with 
scientific objectives and 
implementation strategies for 
future P sustainability, were 
integrated into a single 
framework. This framework 
elucidated the flow characteristics 
and imbalance patterns along the 
life-cycle processes of P , providing 
a foundation for formulating and 
assessing sustainable P strategies. 
It also offers critical insights into 
both established and emerging
 
P-management technologies and 
their synergies. By understanding 
the characteristics and scales of P 
use across systems at various
 
geographical levels, differentiated 
sustainability strategies can be 
developed at the national scale, 
thereby providing essential 
support for the global transition 
toward sustainable P use and 
resource management. 
Global anthropogenic phosphorus (P) cycle changes require active management with 
differentiated sustainability strategies across countries. This study developed a universal 
framework that integrates the comprehensive P flow process with sustainability scenarios 
to assess global anthropogenic P flow patterns and explore future sustainability path -
ways and potential. The results indicated that the global P flow imbalance intensified 
from 1961 to 2022, with over 80% attr
ibuted to croplands. Crucially, global trade 
serves as a significant driving force, redistributing P resources and reinforcing the global 
complexity of P-pollution footprints, driven by 35 key countries. Our model projects
that by 2050, individual measures like reducing fer
tilizer applications, improving feed 
conversion efficiency, and recycling organic fertilizers could save 50% of P and reduce 
P losses by 39%. Crop–livestock integration significantly improved P use efficiency, 
potentially extending the global phosphate rock extraction period by 400 ± 87 y (mean 
± SD). However, due to the low baseline P use efficiency in cropping systems, P recovery 
from waste in developing countries remains limited, necessitating additional measures 
alongside the above high-leverage measures. These findings underscore the indispensable 
role of human consumption and the transformative potential of dietary shifts, which are 
essential given that no country can achieve significant P sustainability gains solely by 
reducing food waste or processing losses. Coordinated supply and demand-side measures 
linking cropping, livestock, and consumption offer key strategies and benefits for system-
atically and differentially advancing P sustainability at both global and national levels.

Human activities have significantly altered natural phosphorus (P) processes within geo-
chemical cycles (1, 2). The decline in global exploitable P reserves, combined with rapidly 
increasing population-driven food demand, makes the sustainable use of this essential 
resource increasingly urgent (the urgency of P sustainability is discussed in SI A
ppendix, 
T ext S1) (3, 4). Changes in the P cycle require proactive management to address the urgent 
challenges of excess P , which causes aquatic pollution ( 3), and scarcity, which affects 
agricultural production and food security (5). These challenges fur
ther underscore the 
need to address P sustainability, particularly b
y reevaluating how effective P management 
strategies can be developed within the socioeconomic development processes ( 6). An 
integrated framework that incorporates P sustainability objectives, informed b
y an 
enhanced understanding of P use characteristics and magnitude across systems and geo-
graphic scales, can provide support for the global transition to sustainable P use and 
management. 
At the national level, the sustainable management of P faces several challenges. In 
industrialized countries, excessive 
fertilizer and manure application has led to low P-use 
efficiency (PUE) (7, 8). Large surpluses of P accumulate in soils, increasing P saturation 
and reducing its crops availability (2
, 9, 10). Many developing countries have an imbal-
anced agricultural P use and crop production. At an early stage, both P fertilizer application 
rates and crop yields are low. In contrast, Brazil, the United Kingdom, China, and India 
are currently experiencing substantial P surplus and low nutrient use efficiencies ( 7, 11, 
12). The extensive P fertilizer applications combined with poor management of human 
and animal waste thr
eatens Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) 6 (Clean Water and 
Sanitation) and SDG 14 (Life Below Water) (13). Inefficient P management disrupts the 
natural P cycle, making P one of the major contributors to the transgression of planetary 
boundaries (
14).

2. Global P Cycle Imbalance Worsens 
2.1. Global P Resource Depletion Is Accelerating. Over the past 
62 y, the global reliance on phosphate rock has steadily increased 
(SI Appendix, Fig. S5). We compiled data on global phosphate rock 
reserves [1.7 × 10 5 million tons (Mt)], distribution, and grades 
(0.25 to 41%) from 1900 to 2020 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). 
The global phosphate rock reserves have been highly concentrated 
over time. Historically, since 1900, approximately 76% of 
phosphate rock reserves have been controlled by five countries 
(SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). As of 2023, this concentration intensified 
further, with eight countries held >90% of the global phosphate 
rock reserves: Morocco (68%), China (5%), Egypt (4%), T unisia 
(3%), Russia (3%), Algeria (3%), Brazil (2%), and South Africa 
(2%) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). From 1998 to 2021, a total of 4.3 
× 103 Mt of phosphate rock was mined globally, yielding 978 
Mt of P2O5 (equivalent to 427 Mt of elemental P) (SI Appendix, 
Fig. S5C). Starting in 1998, phosphate mining and exports in 
major phosphate producing countries, namely, China, M
orocco, 
and the United States, began to increase, with a significant surge 
occurring from 2005 (1.7 × 103 Mt of phosphate rock), peaking 
in 2016 (2.5 × 10 3 Mt of phosphate rock) before declining to 
1.8 × 10 3 Mt of phosphate rock in 2021 ( SI Appendix, Fig. S5 
E–G). The structural shift in global P dynamics is evident in the 
diverging demand trends: while dev
eloped nations experienced 
a decline in phosphate rock and mineral phosphate fertilizer 
demand post-1990, demand in developing countries and emerging 
economies surged. The global center of P production and use is 
shifting toward regions with fast-growing agricultural demand, 
where infrastructur
e is weaker. This shift creates new challenges 
for sustainability (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). 
Although considerable uncertainty remains regarding the 
precise timeline for global phosphate reserve depletion, numer -
ous studies indicate that concerns over the adequacy of global 
phosphate supplies have already emerged, and the system may 
even face the challenge of resource scarcity in the next century 
(25, 26). Considering the limitations and variability of these 
historical estimates, and improvements in data availability since 
earlier projections, we updated this estimation based on the 
latest global phosphate rock reserves data. The most recent sta-
tistics indicate that the remaining economically viable phos -
phate rock reserves, defined as phosphate rock that can be 
economically extracted using existing technology ( 26, 27), 
available for mining amount to only 7.4 × 10 4   Mt of phosphate 
rock (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D ). Under the current growth trend 
in mineral phosphate fertilizer demand, nonr
enewable, gener -
ally available phosphate rock reserves will last for only 120 ± 
26 y (mean ± SD) ( SI Appendix, Fig. S5H ). Considering the 
constraints posed by phosphate rock grades on mining costs, 
technological challenges, and envir
onmental impacts, this esti -
mate is likely low (25, 26).

2.3. Global Trade as a Driver Exacerbating P -Cycle Imbalance. 
The role of global trade in redistributing P resources and pollution 
has continued to intensify, primarily driven by 35 countries 
worldwide (SI  Appendix, Figs.  S11 and S12 ), highlighting its 
profound impact on national-
level P cycling. Overall, the scale 
of the P product trade network expanded continuously from 1990 
to 2022, with total trade volumes of phosphate rock, fertilizers, 
and agricultural products increasing by 23%, 54%, and 256%, 
respectively (Fig.  2 and SI  Appendix, Fig.  S12). Pre -1990, 
phosphate rock trade was dominated by the U
nited States and 
Morocco; post-1990, Russia emerged as a major exporter, Europe 
maintained strong demand (Fig.  2 A and B and SI  Appendix, 
Fig. S11), and the United States shifted from exporter to importer 
amid declining phosphate rock quality and stricter environmental 
regulations (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5K). Between 
1990 and 2020 (Especially since 2008), countries incr
easingly

within the phosphate chemical industry (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), 
consequently prompting nations, such as China in 2017, to reduce

3. Scenario Analysis for Future P Sustainability 
3.1. Global Future P Demand and Potential for System -Wide 
Regulation. In the past half -century, human activities have 
significantly disrupted and altered the global P cy
cle, resulting in 
substantial structural changes in P reserves and flows. Owing to 
the absence of a closed P cycle in agriculture, the next fertilizer 
revolution must prioritize the recycling of nutrients back into the 
agricultural systems (31). Consequently, a sustainable future for P 
will necessitate a reduction in the dependence on phosphate rock 
and the implementation of effective recycling measures across all 
fields of P utilization. In this context, we developed a basic global 
P flow pattern for the 2050 BAU (Business-As-Usual) scenario 
and evaluated the P sustainability potential of 20 specific measures 
(Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S15). 
Our analysis underscores the severe systematic impacts of the 
P losses in agriculture, which drives an unsustainable r
eliance on 
finite mineral P resources and exacerbates environmental pollu -
tion. In the BAU scenario, global demand for P fertilizers is esti-
mated at 26.9 Mt of P , whereas the demand for phosphate rock 
for fertilizer production is projected to be 205.1 Mt (a conservative 
estimate, assuming phosphate rock grades <30% after 2030) (32). 
A minimum of 16.2 Mt of food P is necessary to meet basic 
human needs, and an estimated 69.9 Mt of P will be released into 
the environment. Specifically, P loads in croplands, noncropland 
areas, and surface waters are 36.3 Mt, 19.5 Mt, and 14.1 Mt, 
respectively. The low PUE remains a pervasive and systemic con-
straint. The PUE in the cropping system is 31%, in the livestock 
system 16%, in the aquaculture system 10%, and 31% in the 
consumption system. Cr
ucially, the inefficiency of P use in the 
production sector became even more pronounced when consid -
ering P recovery rates: 17% for the cropping system, 50% for the 
livestock system, and 9% for the consumption system. This indi-
cates that the current P system operates far fr
om its biophysical 
potential, accumulating both resource depletion burden and envi-
ronmental risks. This situation underscores the urgent need to 
explore global P sustainability as there is significant potential for 
improvement. 
This study demonstrates the transformative potential of P syn-
ergistic optimization. Implementing integrated measures (S5) can 
dramatically reduce P input by 97% through phosphate rock and 
chemical P production, while simultaneously achieving substantial 
reductions in P loss (33 to 98%) across the entire social system 
(Fig. 4A). The substitution of mineral P to meet consumption 
needs is achieved through the application of organic fer
tilizers in 
the livestock system (35.4 Mt of P) and the consumption system 
(4.6 Mt of P), resulting in significant P recovery effects, with P 
recovery rate improving by 14 to 186%. Additionally, the PUE 
in the four systems (cropping system at 45%, livestock system at 
23%, aquaculture system at 21%, and consumption system at 
47%) also showed substantial improvements, increasing by 49%, 
42%, 101%, and 49%, respectively. If these measures are imple-
mented globally, P entering croplands is projected to increase by 
17%, whereas 
P in noncroplands and surface waters is expected 
to decrease by 47% and 30%, respectively. The reductions in P 
loss are attributed to all social production subsystems, with the 
detailed loss reduction benefits for each subsystem shown in 
Fig. 4A. Consequently, a cross-sectional approach is essential for 
improving P self-sufficiency and reducing environmental impacts, 
rather than isolated interventions.

4. Conclusion and Perspectives 
The finite and uncontrolled extraction of P resources, inefficient 
utilization, and excessive losses result in structural imbalances within 
the global P cycle. In this context, we developed a comprehensive 
process and sustainability scenario analysis framework to examine 
global anthropogenic P cycling and metabolism from the perspective 
of the entire P flow chain. Overall, this framework enables a dynamic 
assessment of the global anthropogenic P cycle and facilitates the 
identification of key subsystems that drive social P flows and con-
tribute significantly to P losses and environmental impacts. Successful 
implementation of these sustainable strategies depends on global 
cooperation, particularly in terms of technology transfer and knowl-
edge sharing. Therefore, it is essential to developing policies for the 
management of P resources on a global scale, particularly in the areas 
of P resource extraction, trade, and recycling. By establishing a global 
database of P resource flows and creating a platform for international 
cooperation in P management, countries can enhance their under-
standing of global P resources and achieve a coordinated supply of 
P . The International Call for Action on P aims to establish or expand 
an intergovernmental coordination mechanism similar to that devel-
oped for nitrogen. This initiative will assist governments, existing
